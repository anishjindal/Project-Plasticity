Triage

Messages keep landing. The question keeps changing. Sort each one by the question on screen.

Each time the question changes, your brain has to drop one rule and load the other. That switch runs on a network of connections, and like any connection in your brain, it can get stronger with challenging practice. That's neuroplasticity.

Why it matters

Your workday is a switching workout you never planned.

A startup day is a stream of interruptions: a bug, a launch, an investor email. Decades of research show that every switch has a cost.

  1. Every switch costs time, even when you see it coming. Your brain has to reload the rules for the new task.Rogers & Monsell (1995), Journal of Experimental Psychology: General · Read the paper ↗
  2. When you leave a task unfinished, part of your attention stays behind and drags down the next one. Researchers call it attention residue.Leroy (2009), Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes · Read the paper ↗
  3. People who are interrupted make up for it by working faster, but report more stress, frustration and effort.Mark, Gudith & Klocke (2008), CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems · Read the paper ↗
  4. In a Stanford study, heavy media multitaskers were slower to switch tasks and more distracted by irrelevant information than light multitaskers.Ophir, Nass & Wagner (2009), PNAS · Read the paper ↗

Why practice can help

  1. After a few sessions of task-switching training, people's switch costs went down, and the gains carried over to similar switching tasks they hadn't trained on.Karbach & Kray (2009), Developmental Science · Read the paper ↗
  2. The adult brain physically changes with practice. Three months of learning to juggle grew grey matter in areas that process motion, and it partly faded once practice stopped.Draganski et al. (2004), Nature · Read the paper ↗

An honest note. Brain training reliably improves the skills you practise. Evidence that it improves everyday life more broadly is limited, which is why Triage is built from messages like the ones in your own workday. Simons et al. (2016), Psychological Science in the Public Interest · Read the review ↗

It adapts as you go: a little faster after a run of right answers, a little slower after a slip. Aim for steady, not perfect.

Why a clock? Your brain changes most when practice is just beyond comfortable. Too easy and nothing needs to adapt; too hard and you stop learning.

    Why each part of Triage works the way it does.

    1. Switch costYour switch cost is the extra time your brain needs to reload the rules when the question changes. It's the standard measure in task-switching research, and it shrinks with preparation and practice.Rogers & Monsell (1995) · Paper ↗ · Monsell (2003), Trends in Cognitive Sciences · Review ↗
    2. The adaptive clockLearning is fastest when practice is tuned so you succeed most of the time, but not always: about 85% in this model. Triage's clock aims for around 80%.Wilson et al. (2019), Nature Communications · Read the paper ↗
    3. Training the switchRepeated task-switching practice lowered switch costs, and the improvement carried over to similar switching tasks that weren't trained.Karbach & Kray (2009), Developmental Science · Read the paper ↗
    4. SleepSkills practised during the day are consolidated during sleep. Performance can improve after a night's rest, without any extra practice.Walker & Stickgold (2004), Neuron · Read the paper ↗
    5. SpacingSpreading practice over separate days beats cramming it into one session. This review is about memory; the same principle sets how often Triage suggests a round.Cepeda et al. (2006), Psychological Bulletin · Read the paper ↗
    6. What it can't doTraining reliably improves the trained skill. Broad everyday benefits are less certain, so Triage stays close to the switching your real work demands.Simons et al. (2016), Psychological Science in the Public Interest · Read the review ↗
